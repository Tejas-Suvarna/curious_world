The Universe and

◷ Deep time

From first light to oceans

Explore six milestones in order. The dates are approximate, and the stops are spaced evenly for navigation.

13.8 billion years ago

A hot, dense universe expands

As space expanded, the early universe cooled. It would take much longer for stars or planets to appear.

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang

Light can travel freely

Electrons combined with nuclei to form neutral atoms. The light that could then travel through space is visible today as the cosmic microwave background.

Within the first few hundred million years

The first stars begin to shine

Gravity pulled gas together until fusion could begin. The timing of the very first stars remains uncertain.

About 4.6 billion years ago

The Sun and its disk take shape

A cloud of gas and dust collapsed. The Sun grew near the centre, and material in the surrounding disk became the raw ingredients for planets.

About 4.5 billion years ago

Earth grows, and the Moon forms

Rocky bodies collided and built the young Earth. In the leading model, a giant impact sent material into orbit that became the Moon.

By about 4.4 billion years ago

Tiny crystals preserve clues to liquid water

Some ancient zircons from Western Australia carry chemical clues consistent with early liquid water. They give us indirect evidence about a surface that has long since changed.

How a planet came to be

01 / The Big Bang

Space was expanding

Imagine dots on a sheet of stretchy fabric. As the fabric stretches, each dot sees its distant neighbours get farther away. The fabric is an analogy for space itself expanding. Run that expansion backwards and the observable universe was much hotter and denser about 13.8 billion years ago. There is no special dot that marks a centre of expansion.

Light from many distant galaxies reaches us with longer wavelengths, a change called redshift. On large scales, more distant galaxies tend to show more redshift. Astronomers use that pattern as evidence of expansion.

For hundreds of thousands of years, free electrons kept scattering light through the hot early universe. Once things cooled enough for electrons to join nuclei, light could travel much farther. The expansion stretched that light into microwaves. Today we detect it in every direction as the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

02 / Stars and galaxies

Gravity gathers the ingredients

The young universe contained mostly hydrogen and helium. Small differences in density gave gravity a head start. Over time, dark matter gathered into clumps and helped pull gas into them. In some dense pockets, the gas collapsed and heated up until hydrogen began to fuse. The first stars lit up. Groups of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter formed galaxies that grew over time, sometimes by merging.

Inside stars, fusion builds elements such as carbon and oxygen. Dying stars can send those elements back into space, where they become part of later clouds. The oxygen you breathe and the carbon in your body came from material that passed through earlier generations of stars.

Light takes time to reach us, so a telescope can look back in time. The faint galaxies in Webb’s Deep Field include some whose light left long before Earth existed. The bright arcs you see are light from more distant galaxies bent by a galaxy cluster in the foreground.

03 / The Solar System

A spinning disk builds planets

About 4.6 billion years ago, gravity pulled together part of a cloud of gas and dust. As it shrank, it spun faster, like a skater pulling in their arms. Most of the mass collected in the centre, where the Sun formed. The material orbiting it spread into a protoplanetary disk.

Tiny solid grains stuck together. As some clumps grew into larger bodies called planetesimals, their gravity helped them gather more material. This growth by addition is called accretion. Some collisions built larger worlds, while others broke them apart.

Close to the Sun, heat made it hard for many ices to remain solid. Rock and metal supplied the ingredients for Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. Farther away, colder conditions let ices add to the supply of solids. Across the outer Solar System, Jupiter and Saturn became gas giants, while Uranus and Neptune became ice giants. Asteroids and comets preserve material from that formative era.

04 / Earth and the Moon

A world takes shape through collisions

Earth grew from many rocky building blocks. Impacts and radioactive decay supplied heat, allowing dense iron-rich material to sink towards the centre. Lighter rocky material remained above it. That separation, called differentiation, helped form a metal-rich core and a rocky mantle. A crust formed and changed as the outer parts cooled.

How do we date a world that keeps recycling its rocks? Some meteorites preserve ancient Solar System material. Scientists measure the proportions of radioactive elements and the products of their decay. Combining that evidence with lead measurements from Earth puts its age at about 4.54 billion years.

In the leading giant impact hypothesis, a large body often called Theia struck the young Earth. Some material reached orbit and gathered to form the Moon. Lunar rocks record intense early heating, and some of their chemical signatures are strikingly similar to Earth’s. Scientists still investigate how the impact unfolded and how quickly the Moon assembled.

05 / The young Earth

From hot rock to a watery surface

The Hadean is the name for Earth's earliest stretch of geologic history. Early Earth was hot, and large impacts could melt parts of its surface. Volcanoes and cooling rock released gases, including water vapour and carbon dioxide. This process is called outgassing. The atmosphere changed over time and contained very little free oxygen compared with today's air.

Where and when the surface cooled enough, water vapour could condense and liquid water could collect. Some of Earth's water may also have arrived in water-bearing material as the planet grew. Scientists are still working out the balance between sources.

There are few surviving rocks from this time. But some zircons, tough mineral grains from the Jack Hills in Western Australia, date back about 4.4 billion years. Some preserve chemical signatures consistent with material altered by liquid water. That makes them clues to early water, with open questions about how widespread it was.

◇ Think like a geologist

What can one crystal tell you?

Suppose a 4.4-billion-year-old zircon carries signs that its parent material had interacted with water. What can we infer about when water existed?

Reveal the reasoning

Liquid water likely existed by then. The crystal alone gives us little information about the depth or extent of any early oceans.

✦ Revision notes

Five ideas to take with you

  • Expansion: Redshift and ancient microwave light help reconstruct the early universe.
  • Stars: Gravity gathers matter, and fusion creates many of the elements later worlds inherit.
  • Solar System: A spinning disk supplied the ingredients for planets to grow by accretion.
  • Earth and Moon: Meteorites help date Earth. Lunar rocks help scientists assess the giant impact model.
  • Early Earth: Gases escaped from hot rock, liquid water gathered as conditions changed, and zircons preserve clues.
